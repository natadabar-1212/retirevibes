Why Victoria might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

The only Canadian city where you can garden year-round — combined with a compact, walkable downtown, whale-watching from the harbour wall, and English as the only language you need.

🌸

Canada's gentlest climate — by far

Victoria averages just 25 centimetres of snow per year — most winters bring none at all. Daffodils bloom in February. The average January high is 9°C (48°F), and summers rarely crack 25°C. For Canadians, this sounds impossible. For Americans used to mild weather, it feels like the Pacific Northwest at its most livable — without Portland's rain.

🐋

Nature you don't have to travel for

Orca pods pass through the Strait of Juan de Fuca all summer — you can watch them from shore or book a Zodiac tour in 20 minutes. Butchart Gardens, one of the most famous horticultural sites in North America, is a 20-minute drive. Tofino's dramatic surf beaches are three hours on a road trip you'll want to take more than once.

🚶

A city built for living without a car

The Inner Harbour, Beacon Hill Park, and the old residential neighborhoods of Fairfield and James Bay are all connected by a walkable grid. The downtown stretch along Government Street has everything — independent bookshops, farmers' markets, excellent coffee. Many retirees here sell their car within the first year and don't miss it.

🍷

A genuine food and arts scene

Victoria punches above its weight culturally — the Belfry Theatre, the Victoria Symphony, an arts scene driven in part by the University of Victoria, and a restaurant culture that takes local and seasonal seriously. The Cowichan Valley wine region is an hour away, producing cool-climate Pinot Noir and Chardonnay that rival Oregon's Willamette Valley.

What a Tuesday in Victoria feels like.

Morning light comes through the bedroom window and you notice, not for the first time, that the light here has a particular quality — soft and silvery, filtered through sea air. Coffee on the back porch. The garden is doing something in late February that no garden you've ever owned would attempt: a crocus, definitely, and what looks like the beginning of a daffodil.

Mid-morning walk down to the Inner Harbour — past the Empress Hotel, past the causeway buskers, past the float planes bobbing at the dock. A flat white at a café on Government Street. Someone at the next table is a retired teacher from Seattle who moved here eleven years ago and claims it was the best decision of her life. This is a common story in Victoria.

Afternoon at Beacon Hill Park — the peacocks are out, because of course there are peacocks, that's just Victoria. A swim at Crystal Pool or a long read on the grass. Dinner at a Fairfield restaurant where you booked a table because you live here now and you do things like book tables at neighborhood restaurants. The evening is unhurried and the walk home is lit with the kind of soft Pacific dusk that makes everything look slightly enchanted.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Visa & Residency

6 months visa-free, then it's complex

US citizens can enter Canada for up to 6 months without a visa — technically a "visitor record." Long-term retirement in Canada has no dedicated retirement visa path. The most practical routes for Americans are: family sponsorship (if you have a Canadian spouse or adult child citizen), the Federal Skilled Worker stream (Express Entry), or Provincial Nominee Programs if you have skills in demand. Expect 12–24 months minimum for any PR pathway. Consult a Canadian immigration attorney early.

Healthcare

Provincial MSP — world-class, with a wait

Once you're a BC resident, you qualify for the Medical Services Plan (MSP) — BC's provincial health insurance. It covers hospital and physician services with no premiums (premiums were eliminated in 2020). The catch: most provinces have a 3-month waiting period for new residents. Get comprehensive private health insurance to bridge that gap. After enrollment, BC's healthcare system is excellent — Victoria has two major hospitals and a well-staffed specialist community.

Taxes

BC has no sales tax on groceries or rent

BC's provincial income tax is progressive and stacks on top of federal Canadian tax. As a US citizen living in Canada, you're still required to file US federal taxes — but the US-Canada tax treaty largely prevents double taxation. Canada has no capital gains exemption equivalent to the US primary home sale exclusion, so talk to a cross-border CPA before selling US property after establishing Canadian residency. BC has no provincial sales tax on food, prescription drugs, or residential rent.
