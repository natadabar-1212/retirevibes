Why People Choose Ambergris Caye

The vibe in four cards

Ambergris Caye self-selects. Not everyone wants this: a small island, one main street, golf carts instead of cars, limited infrastructure, and a Caribbean pace that cannot be hurried. But the people who want exactly this tend to really, really want it.

🐠
The Mesoamerican Barrier Reef — 300 meters offshore
The Mesoamerican Barrier Reef is the second-largest coral reef system in the world after the Great Barrier Reef, stretching 600 miles from Mexico's Yucatán to Honduras. Ambergris Caye sits inside the reef's lagoon — the reef wall runs 300 meters offshore, reachable by a 10-minute boat ride from San Pedro's dock. The snorkeling and diving range from beginner-accessible to world-class: nurse sharks and ray feeding at Shark Ray Alley, wall dives at Hol Chan Marine Reserve, and pelagic action at the Blue Hole (an hour offshore). For anyone who has ever wanted to live where serious snorkeling or diving is a casual Thursday activity, this is one of the few places in the Americas where that's actually true.
🗣️
English is Belize's official language
Belize is the only country in Central America where English is the official language — a legacy of British colonization (the country was called British Honduras until 1973). All government business, legal documents, medical care, and commerce are conducted in English. Belizean Creole is widely spoken locally, but standard English is universally understood. This matters practically: real estate contracts are in English, doctors speak English, pharmacies label medications in English, and the legal system operates on English common law principles that Americans and Canadians find more familiar than Latin American civil law systems. The language barrier that complicates retirement in most of Latin America and Asia simply doesn't exist in Belize.
🛺
Golf carts and island pace
San Pedro, Ambergris Caye's main town, does not have conventional cars — the streets are unpaved sand lanes navigated by golf carts, bicycles, and occasional motorcycles. This is not a quirk; it defines the character of daily life. There are no traffic jams, no road rage, no car payments. Distances are small: most of what you need in San Pedro — grocery stores, restaurants, pharmacies, the post office, the ATMs — is within a 10-minute golf cart ride. A golf cart rental runs $50–70/day or $400–600/month. The island is 25 miles long but most expats live in or near San Pedro town and move primarily within a 3-mile zone. If you want a genuinely car-free life with Caribbean water outside your door, this is one of the few places in the Americas where that's structurally available.
💱
Dollar-pegged currency and QRP visa
The Belizean dollar (BZD) has been pegged 2:1 to the US dollar since 1978 — meaning BZD 2 always equals USD 1. There are no currency fluctuation surprises, no exchange rate anxiety, and no complex conversion math. USD is widely accepted throughout Belize, especially in tourist areas. Belize's Qualified Retired Person (QRP) visa requires $2,000/month in pension, Social Security, or retirement income (from a foreign source), a $1,000 application fee, and $300/year in annual fees. Once approved, you're exempt from Belizean income taxes on foreign-sourced income and can import a car and household goods duty-free once each. It's one of the more accessible formal retirement visas in the Caribbean region.

Life on the Ground

A Friday on the island

You live in a 2BR apartment on the lagoon side — $1,400/month, ceiling fans, a deck where pelicans land sometimes, a golf cart parked below. You moved from Houston. The commute from the bedroom to the coffee maker is the longest drive you make most days.

Friday morning: the dive boat leaves the dock at 8am. Hol Chan Marine Reserve — 30 minutes south by boat. You've been living here eight months and you still can't quite believe the reef is this close. Nurse sharks at Shark Ray Alley; a loggerhead sea turtle in the seagrass on the way back. You're back on land by noon with snorkeling gear dripping on your deck.

Afternoon: golf cart to Estel's for lunch — eggs, rice and beans, fresh-squeezed orange juice, $11. A friend from Montreal arrived Tuesday; you're showing him the spots. Slow afternoon: reading, napping, watching the lagoon. This is a legitimate use of a Friday here. Nobody is judging you.

Evening: dinner at a spot on the beach — fresh whole snapper grilled, $28 for two, eaten with your feet basically in the sand. The Caribbean is 82°F in October. The reef is 300 meters away. You know exactly how lucky you are.

The Numbers

What it actually costs

Ambergris Caye is not the cheapest Caribbean destination — island logistics, imported goods, and a tourist-driven economy mean costs are higher than mainland Belize or mainland Latin America. It is, however, significantly less than comparable Caribbean islands with US infrastructure.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Belize is straightforward for Americans — English, familiar legal system, dollar parity. The main things to understand are the QRP visa process, healthcare limitations, and what island infrastructure actually means.

🛂
The QRP visa
Belize's Qualified Retired Person (QRP) program is administered by the Belize Tourism Board. To qualify: you must be at least 45 years old, have a minimum of $2,000/month in pension, Social Security, annuity, or qualified retirement income from a foreign source. Application fee is $1,000; annual renewal fee is $300. Benefits: exemption from all Belize taxes on foreign-sourced income, one-time duty-free import of household goods, and one duty-free vehicle import. QRP holders cannot work for a Belizean employer (but can own a business). The application process involves submitting financial documents, a police record, and medical clearance — typically processed in 3–6 months. Unlike some retirement visas, QRP does not automatically lead to permanent residency; that requires a separate residency application after 5 years.
🏥
Healthcare — understand the limits
Healthcare on Ambergris Caye is limited — San Pedro has a small public polyclinic and several private GP clinics for routine care. For anything serious — specialist consultations, surgery, imaging, emergency care — you'll need to get to Belize City (a 30-minute flight on a puddle-jumper or 90-min water taxi) or Chetumal, Mexico (a 2-hour ferry and drive). Karl Heusner Memorial Hospital in Belize City is the national referral hospital; quality is inconsistent by US standards. Many expats on Ambergris Caye carry international health insurance that covers medical evacuation to Houston or Miami for serious conditions — this is strongly recommended. For routine care, the island's clinics handle most general practice, dental, and minor emergencies reasonably well. Plan your insurance accordingly.
⛈️
Hurricane season — a real factor
Belize sits in the Caribbean hurricane belt. The formal hurricane season runs June 1 through November 30, with peak activity in August, September, and October. Ambergris Caye has been directly impacted by major storms: Hurricane Iris in 2001 caused significant damage; tropical systems regularly affect the island with flooding, wind, and utility outages. Most full-time expats leave for 4–8 weeks during peak hurricane season (August–October), returning when the risk drops. This is worth factoring into your lifestyle and housing plans: you'll want a lease that accommodates seasonal absence, buildings constructed to withstand wind and flooding, and a concrete exit plan for when a storm approaches. This is not a reason not to live in Belize — it's a reality that any island Caribbean retirement requires accounting for.
