🇬🇪 Tbilisi, Georgia

365-day visa-free, 8,000 years of wine, and the Caucasus at your door

Georgia — the country, not the US state — grants American citizens 365-day visa-free entry, renewable simply by leaving and returning. Tbilisi is its capital: a city of Persian-influenced wooden balconies, sulfur bath district, ancient churches, and a food and wine culture that is one of the world's great undiscovered pleasures. The Georgian alphabet, the polyphonic choral tradition, the qvevri wine (fermented in clay amphorae buried underground) — this is a civilization 3,000 years old that most Americans know nothing about. At $1,000–2,200/month for a couple, it's also among the most cost-effective options in any European-adjacent destination.

Why People Choose Tbilisi

The vibe in four cards

Tbilisi is for people who want something genuinely different — a civilization that doesn't fit Western European or Asian categories, with extraordinary hospitality, an 8,000-year wine culture, and costs that make almost everything else look expensive.

🍷
The birthplace of wine — 8,000 years of qvevri tradition
Archaeological evidence places winemaking in the South Caucasus — modern-day Georgia — at approximately 6,000 BCE, making Georgia the oldest documented wine-producing region on earth. Georgian wine culture is built around the qvevri (ქვევრი) — a large clay amphora buried underground — in which both white and red grapes are fermented with their skins for several months, producing wines with a distinctive amber color, tannins, and a complexity unlike anything in French, Italian, or Spanish winemaking. The Rkatsiteli and Kisi grape varieties produce amber wines of genuine character. Saperavi (the red) produces some of the most distinctive and age-worthy wines in the world at prices that would be considered absurd in Burgundy or Napa. A bottle of excellent Georgian natural wine at a Tbilisi wine bar: $8–20. A glass at a restaurant: $3–7. The wine culture here is not tourist-facing — it is woven into every meal, every toast (the tamada, or toastmaster, leads ritualized toasts at Georgian feasts that can last hours), every family occasion. For serious wine people, Tbilisi is a revelation.
🏛️
Old Tbilisi — balconies, baths, and Abanotubani
Old Tbilisi (Dzveli Tbilisi) is characterized by its wooden balconied houses — ornately carved latticework overhanging narrow streets — a Persian-influenced architectural tradition unique to the city. The neighborhood climbs steep hillsides above the Mtkvari (Kura) River, with ancient churches (Metekhi Church, 5th century; Narikala Fortress ruins, 4th century; Sioni Cathedral, 6th century) visible from across the city. Abanotubani (bath district) is the sulfur spring neighborhood where domed bathhouses have operated since at least the 5th century — the name "Tbilisi" derives from the Georgian word for "warm" (tbili), referencing these natural hot springs. A private room at a sulfur bathhouse for two runs $15–30/hour. King Vakhtang Gorgasali (the founder of Tbilisi, 5th century) reportedly chose the city's location after a pheasant he hunted fell into a hot spring and emerged cooked. True or not, the sulfur baths are the oldest fact about Tbilisi, and they still work exactly as advertised.
🥩
Georgian food — a feast culture unlike any other
Georgian cuisine is one of the world's great overlooked food cultures — a crossroads of Persian, Ottoman, and Caucasian influences producing a table of extraordinary richness. Khachapuri (cheese bread, with multiple regional forms — the Adjarian version, boat-shaped and topped with a raw egg and butter, is the most theatrical); khinkali (dumplings filled with spiced meat and broth, eaten by holding the top knot and biting carefully); satsivi (poultry in walnut sauce); pkhali (walnut-herb-vegetable balls); badrijani nigvzit (fried eggplant with walnut paste and pomegranate). A full supra (Georgian feast) at a restaurant, with multiple dishes, wine, and chacha (grape pomace brandy): $20–35 for two. The Georgian concept of hospitality (სტუმარი — stumari, "guest") considers the guest a gift from God; this is not a metaphor. Foreigners who are invited to Georgian homes or who become regulars at local restaurants describe the experience of Georgian hospitality as one of the most unexpected and affecting things they've encountered abroad.
⛰️
Caucasus mountains — Kazbegi and beyond
The Greater Caucasus mountains begin 150km north of Tbilisi — one of the most dramatic mountain ranges in the world, with peaks exceeding 5,000m. Kazbegi (Stepantsminda), 2,200m elevation in the Greater Caucasus, is 2.5 hours by marshrutka (shared minivan) from Tbilisi. The Gergeti Trinity Church, perched on a promontory above the town at 2,170m with 5,047m Mount Kazbek behind it, is one of the most photographed sites in the Caucasus and one of the most extraordinary views in Europe. The Military Highway connecting Tbilisi to Vladikavkaz passes through the Daryal Gorge and is a day trip from the city. Borjomi (mineral water, alpine forest, hiking), Sighnaghi (wine country village in the Alazani Valley), and Vardzia (12th-century cave monastery carved into a volcanic cliff) are all within day-trip or overnight distance. For people who want urban life with immediate mountain access, Tbilisi's geography delivers dramatically.

Life on the Ground

A Sunday between the baths and the wine bar

You rent a 2BR apartment in the Vera neighborhood — $600/month, third floor of a Soviet-era building with a new renovation, 10-minute walk to Old Town. You moved from Austin. The comparison that made you move: Austin's rent, Tbilisi's everything else.

9am: Abanotubani. You have a private sulfur bath room for two hours — $20. The sulfur water is 37°C and slightly milky with minerals; you have read that people have bathed in these springs for 1,500 years and you believe it. You emerge soft-skinned and extremely calm.

Late morning: the Dry Bridge flea market. Soviet-era paintings, Georgian silver work, antique chess sets with hand-carved pieces, old carpets. You buy a small oil painting of the Narikala fortress for $35. You have bought too many small oil paintings.

Afternoon: wine bar in Fabrika (a repurposed Soviet sewing factory now housing restaurants, cafes, and studios). An Rkatsiteli amber wine from Kakheti — $5 a glass. A khinkali plate — 8 dumplings, $4. Dinner later at a neighborhood Georgian restaurant: full supra spread, Saperavi by the carafe, chacha to finish. $22 for two. You are in bed by 10pm. Tbilisi is good on Sundays.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Georgia's 365-day visa-free policy for Americans is the most permissive entry policy of any European-adjacent country. The practical considerations are around the geopolitical context, healthcare, and long-term residency structure.

🛂
365-day visa-free — and what comes after
US citizens can enter Georgia and stay up to 365 days without a visa — this is one of the world's most generous visa-free policies for Americans. The stay renews by leaving (even briefly — many long-term residents cross to Armenia or Turkey for a day) and returning. For permanent residency or longer structured stay, Georgia offers the Article 17 Residence Permit (also called the "Investor" or general category residence permit), which requires establishing economic activity in Georgia or demonstrating passive income. A Temporary Residence Permit based on property ownership is also available; purchasing property worth GEL 100,000+ (approximately $36,000) qualifies. Work permits are available for employed expats. The long-term residency pathway is less formalized than Portugal's D7 but workable — many long-term American residents in Tbilisi operate on rolling 365-day entries for years. Georgia's E-residency program for business registration adds a second layer of official connection. Consult a Georgia-based immigration attorney for current pathway advice, as regulations evolve.
⚠️
Geopolitical context — honest assessment
Georgia borders Russia to the north and has two Russian-occupied territories (South Ossetia and Abkhazia) within its internationally recognized borders since the 2008 war. This is a fact of Georgia's situation that requires acknowledgment. The ongoing Russia-Ukraine conflict has increased geopolitical attention on the broader region. Within Tbilisi proper, the security situation for foreign residents has been stable and everyday life proceeds normally. However: the proximity to an active geopolitical fault line is different from the situation in, say, Portugal or Panama, and this requires honest personal assessment. The US State Department maintains a travel advisory for Georgia; check it and read the specifics (which vary by region within the country) before planning. Many thousands of Western expats live in Tbilisi without significant security concerns; the geopolitical context is a background consideration rather than an immediate daily risk, but it is not zero and should not be treated as such in personal planning.
🏥
Healthcare in Tbilisi
Tbilisi has a reasonable private healthcare sector for a city of 1.1 million — the Tbilisi Heart and Vascular Clinic, Aversi Clinic, and New Hospital are among the main private facilities with modern equipment and some English-speaking staff. For routine, urgent, and moderately complex care, Tbilisi's private clinics are adequate. For serious complex procedures — advanced cardiac surgery, cancer treatment requiring high-technology equipment, neurosurgery — most expats travel to Istanbul (2 hours by air, $100–180 round trip), which has excellent private hospital infrastructure, or to Germany, Poland, or other EU destinations. Medical evacuation insurance is standard practice for Tbilisi expats. Private health insurance covering Georgia with medical evacuation to Istanbul/EU: approximately $1,800–3,600/year for a couple in their 50s. US Medicare does not cover Georgia. A private GP consultation in Tbilisi: $30–60. Dental: significantly below US rates — a crown typically runs $150–300.
