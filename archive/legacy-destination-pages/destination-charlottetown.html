Why Charlottetown might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

Canada's most affordable provincial capital — an island province with the most affordable housing of any Canadian city its size, red sand beaches that look painted, and a community so small everyone eventually knows your name.

🏖️

Red sand beaches — actually red

PEI's beaches are famous for their red sandstone cliffs and red-tinged sand — iron oxide in the sandstone creates one of the most distinctive coastal landscapes in North America. Cavendish Beach is the famous one, but the island has 800km of coastline to explore. Brackley Beach, Greenwich, Basin Head. The water is warm enough to swim in (relatively, by Canadian standards) from July through September.

🦞

Lobster suppers are an institution, not a tourist trap

PEI lobster suppers — whole lobster, chowder, mussels, fresh bread, dessert — are a genuine local tradition that predates tourism by decades. New Glasgow Lobster Suppers, St. Ann's Church Lobster Suppers, the Northshore Fisherman's Wharf — these aren't restaurants, they're community halls that happen to serve extraordinary seafood. A full supper, lobster included, runs well under $60 CAD. This is daily life on PEI, not a special occasion.

🎭

A cultural city punching well above its size

Charlottetown is the birthplace of Canadian Confederation (1864 Charlottetown Conference) and takes that history seriously. The Confederation Centre of the Arts is a major national arts complex — including Anne of Green Gables: The Musical, which has run for 50+ years. The PEI Brewing Company, the Charlottetown Farmers' Market, and an emerging restaurant scene have added to a city that feels significantly more alive than its population of 40,000 would suggest.

💰

The most affordable housing in Canada

PEI consistently has the lowest average home prices of any Canadian province — about $360K USD for a 2-bedroom in Charlottetown, versus $520K in Ottawa, $620K in Victoria, or $820K in Vancouver. If your retirement planning requires homeownership in Canada, PEI is where the math is most likely to work. The island's small scale means you're also never more than 30 minutes from anywhere on PEI.

What a Sunday on PEI feels like.

Morning: the Charlottetown Farmers' Market runs year-round — a converted 19th-century building with local oysters, farm vegetables, island cheeses, and a cranberry wine you didn't know you needed. You buy too much. The person at the next booth knows the person you bought the cheese from. This is the scale of PEI: everything is connected.

Afternoon: drive to the north shore — 30 minutes — to Brackley Beach in PEI National Park. The beach is wide and backed by dunes. In July, the water is warm enough to swim. The red cliffs are genuinely that color, not filtered. You walk for an hour without seeing anyone you didn't already know was there. In the late afternoon light, the sand turns a deeper amber.

Evening: lobster supper at a church hall 20 minutes east of the city. Whole lobster, chowder, mussels, homemade rolls, lemon meringue pie. The people at the next table are from PEI. They explain that they come every summer because it's that good. You ask them why they live here. They look at each other and seem mildly confused by the question, as if the island explained itself.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Residency

PEI PNP — small province, focused pathways

The PEI Provincial Nominee Program has a few distinct streams — including the Labour Impact category and the Express Entry stream. PEI is one of the smaller provinces and actively recruits newcomers to maintain population growth. As with all Canadian immigration, there's no dedicated retirement visa — economic immigration categories are the primary routes. Work with a licensed RCIC who knows PEI's specific programs; the province's small size means the program teams are accessible and responsive compared to larger provinces.

Healthcare

Good basics — plan for Halifax for complex needs

PEI Health covers physician and hospital services through Queen Elizabeth Hospital. The island is well-served for primary and emergency care. For complex specialties — cardiac surgery, advanced oncology, certain orthopedic procedures — patients are typically referred to Halifax's QEII Health Sciences Centre, a 2.5-hour drive. The Confederation Bridge connects PEI to New Brunswick year-round. Private supplemental insurance covers dental, vision, and prescription drugs not covered by provincial plan.

Island life realities

Understand what "island" actually means

PEI is connected to the mainland by the Confederation Bridge (12.9km, $50 CAD one-way toll). There's also a ferry from Wood Islands to Pictou, Nova Scotia (May–December). The island is small — 280km end to end — and Charlottetown is its only real city. Shopping options, specialist services, and cultural events are limited compared to Halifax or Ottawa. Many island residents make regular mainland trips for major purchases or appointments. This is the trade-off for the lifestyle and the price.

The "is this real" side

Plan a scouting trip

Come in summer — July and August — to see PEI at its best: beaches, lobster suppers, the Festival season at Confederation Centre. But also explore in late September or October to understand what shoulder season looks like. A February visit will tell you what winters actually feel like. Island life has a specific rhythm; you want to know it before you commit.
