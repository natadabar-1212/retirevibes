🇭🇺 Budapest, Hungary

Thermal baths, the Danube at night,
and one of Europe's great bargains

Budapest is the kind of city that stops people mid-sentence when they see it for the first time — the Parliament lit up across the Danube, the Chain Bridge, the Art Nouveau Széchenyi Baths, the ruin bars of the Jewish Quarter. It's a full-scale European capital at Central European prices, with culture, food, and healthcare that punch well above what the costs suggest.

Why People Choose Budapest

The vibe in four cards

Budapest rewards the people who see past the tourist version of it — who take a membership at Széchenyi rather than buying a day pass, who find the neighborhood ruin bar rather than the famous one, who walk Buda rather than staying in Pest. That Budapest is extraordinary.

♨️
Thermal bath culture — built into daily life
Budapest sits atop more than 100 natural hot springs, giving it the title "City of Spas." The Széchenyi Baths (opened 1913) in City Park is the largest medicinal bath complex in Europe — outdoor pools at 38°C, indoor neoclassical halls, steam rooms, and a social culture that makes lingering for three hours completely normal. Gellért Baths, Rudas (with a rooftop pool overlooking the Danube), and Király (operating since 1565, built during Ottoman rule) each have distinct architectural characters. A monthly membership at Széchenyi runs approximately $50. Many expats go weekly. The thermal bath is not a tourist attraction here — it's infrastructure. A city where this is part of ordinary life requires a genuine adjustment in what you expect from Tuesday afternoon.
🏛️
The Danube and architectural grandeur
The Budapest Parliament — built 1885–1904, modeled loosely on Westminster — is one of the most impressive buildings in Europe and is best seen lit up across the Danube at night from Fisherman's Bastion on the Buda side. The entire stretch of the Danube banks (from Margaret Bridge to Petőfi Bridge, including the Parliament, Buda Castle, Gellért Hill, and the Chain Bridge) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The city is genuinely split into two: Pest (flat, commercial, restaurant-dense, nightlife) and Buda (hilly, residential, quieter, with spectacular views). Most expats live in Pest; most spend time in both. The Castle District, Andrássy Avenue (Budapest's answer to the Champs-Élysées), the Opera House — this is a city of genuine architectural ambition that survived more or less intact.
🍺
Ruin bars and the social scene
Budapest's ruin bar phenomenon started in the early 2000s when young Hungarians began occupying derelict buildings in the Jewish Quarter (District VII) and turning them into bar complexes. Szimpla Kert is the most famous — a rambling multi-room space in a former factory, with mismatched furniture, plant life overtaking everything, and live music in corners. But the ruin bar concept has spread throughout the city and generated a broader culture of inventive, affordable nightlife that the expat community plugs into naturally. Beyond the ruin bars: Budapest has a well-established jazz scene, an opera house that charges €15–40 for tickets to world-class performances, and a Hungarian film and theater culture that is accessible even without language fluency.
🥩
Hungarian food and Tokaj wine
Hungarian cuisine is a serious tradition — not a tourist-facing performance of it. Gulyás (goulash) in its proper form is a beef and paprika soup, not the thick stew often exported as "Hungarian." Lángos (deep-fried flatbread with sour cream and cheese) from street vendors; pörkölt (paprika meat stew served with nokedli dumplings); halászlé (fisherman's soup from the Danube); chimney cake (kürtőskalács) from the Christmas markets. The Central Market Hall (Nagy Vásárcsarnok) on the Pest riverfront is a 1897 iron-and-tile food market that operates as both tourist attraction and real food source. Tokaj — Hungary's wine region in the northeast — produces some of the world's great dessert wines (Aszú), but also excellent dry Furmint white wines that pair with the local food culture. A bottle of good Tokaj in a Budapest wine bar costs $8–15.

Life on the Ground

A Friday between Buda and Pest

You rent a 2BR apartment in District VI near Andrássy Avenue — $800/month, second floor, high ceilings, original 1910 parquet floors, a balcony that fits two chairs. You moved from Denver. Denver's winters are colder than Budapest's but Denver does not have Széchenyi Baths three kilometers away.

Friday afternoon: Széchenyi at 3pm. You have a monthly membership. You sit in the outdoor thermal pool for an hour in November — the water is 38°C, the air is 4°C, and steam rises around you. Other people are playing chess in the pool. This is still your favorite fact about Budapest.

Across the river at 6pm: walk up to Fisherman's Bastion on the Buda side. The Parliament lit across the Danube. You've been here seven months and you still stop and look for a minute. Some things don't normalize.

Evening: dinner in the Jewish Quarter at a restaurant that opened two years ago and already has a Michelin Bib Gourmand. Modern Hungarian — duck liver, lentil cream, Tokaj wine reduction. €32 for two with wine. Back across the Chain Bridge on foot at 11pm, lit up over the river. Budapest at night in the cold is one of the finest things.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Hungary's residency situation for non-EU nationals requires careful navigation — the options have changed in recent years.

🛂
Residency for Americans — the current options
Hungary is a Schengen member, which means US citizens can stay 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa — the same limit that applies across the Schengen zone. For longer stays, Hungary introduced the Guest Investor Program in 2024, which grants a 10-year renewable residence permit in exchange for a qualifying investment (€250,000 in approved Hungarian investment funds). This is the main new pathway for non-EU nationals of means. Hungary also offers various D-category national visas (for employment, study, or specific categories), but a straightforward income-based retirement visa comparable to Portugal's D7 or Spain's NLV does not currently exist. The visa situation in Hungary is less favorable for ordinary retired Americans than Iberian Peninsula options. Many expats in Budapest maintain their stay via periodic travel (leaving and re-entering the Schengen zone) while pursuing longer-term residency through other channels. Consult an immigration specialist with current Hungarian practice before planning around Budapest as a long-term base.
🏥
Healthcare in Budapest
Budapest has strong private healthcare infrastructure, particularly the Medicover network — a Central European private hospital group with multiple Budapest locations, English-speaking physicians, and modern equipment. Semmelweis University Hospital and the Na Homolce Hospital are major public academic centers of high quality. Private consultation at Medicover or similar facilities runs €50–120 for GP visits; specialist consultations €80–200. Hungary's public healthcare is available to legal residents who pay into the system, but non-residents on tourist stays pay out of pocket. Comprehensive international health insurance covering Budapest costs $2,500–5,000/year for a couple in their 50s. For more complex procedures — particularly oncology or cardiac surgery — many expats prefer to travel to Vienna (45 minutes by train) or access care through medical travel insurance. US Medicare does not cover Budapest.
❄️
Winter — it's real, and beautiful
Budapest has four genuine seasons, and winter is genuinely cold — average January temperatures around 2°C (36°F), with periods below freezing, occasional snow, and short days. This is not a mild European winter; it's a Central European winter. The people who love Budapest in winter tend to be those who find cold-and-beautiful compelling: the city lit up at night, the Christmas markets along Vörösmarty Square, the thermal baths in outdoor pools with snow on the surrounding stonework, the opera and concert season in full swing, the restaurants and cafes operating at full indoor warmth. The people who struggle are those who planned to be outdoors most of the time. Budapest rewards those with an indoor-and-thermal-bath approach to winter, and punishes those who expected Mediterranean weather.
