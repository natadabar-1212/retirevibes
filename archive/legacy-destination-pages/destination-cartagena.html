Why People Choose Cartagena

The vibe in four cards

Cartagena is not a typical expat destination — it's a deeply Colombian city with a specific, layered cultural identity that rewards the people who engage with it rather than sit apart from it.

🏰
The Walled City — one of the Americas' finest
Cartagena's Ciudad Amurallada (Walled City) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the best-preserved colonial centers in the Western Hemisphere. The walls, built between the 16th and 18th centuries to protect against pirate raids and European rivals, still enclose a living neighborhood of cobblestone streets, plazas, churches, mansions, and boutique hotels. The architecture reflects the collision of Spanish colonial design and African cultural influence — colors that don't exist in other cities, proportions that feel both grand and intimate. Walking it at 6am, before the tourists arrive, is one of the great experiences in Latin America.
🌊
Caribbean coast and islands nearby
Cartagena sits on the Caribbean, and the Rosario Islands — a coral archipelago 45 minutes by boat — are within easy reach for snorkeling, diving, and day trips. The beaches in Cartagena proper (Bocagrande, Marbella) are more urban than pristine; the better beaches require a boat or short travel. The Caribbean water is warm (84°F) year-round. For those who want a mix of colonial city life with genuine beach access, Cartagena's location on the coast makes this combination possible without choosing between them.
🎶
Afro-Caribbean culture and music
Cartagena's cultural identity is deeply shaped by its African heritage — the majority of the population in the historic city and surrounding communities descends from enslaved Africans brought to the coast during the colonial era. This gives the city its particular music (cumbia and vallenato have roots here), its food (sancocho, arepas de huevo, cocadas), its festivals, and its social energy. The annual Hay Festival brings international literary figures; the Afro film festival celebrates African diasporic cinema. For retirees who want cultural engagement rather than a resort bubble, Cartagena rewards genuine curiosity.
💵
Colombia's Pensionado visa — one of the easiest
Colombia's Pensionado (retirement) visa requires proof of a pension or retirement income of approximately $700/month — one of the lowest income thresholds for permanent-residency-eligible visas in Latin America. The visa grants a 3-year renewable residence permit; after 5 years of continuous residence, you're eligible to apply for permanent residency. Unlike some Latin American countries, Colombia's immigration process is well-organized, the requirements are transparent, and immigration attorneys in Cartagena and Bogotá handle the process routinely for $500–800 in fees. The exchange rate (approximately COP 4,100 per USD) means US dollars go significantly further than the headline numbers suggest.

Life on the Ground

A Saturday inside the walls

You live in a 2BR apartment in the Getsemaní neighborhood, just outside the old city walls — $900/month, high ceilings, a small terrace, a mural on the wall across the street that changes every six months. You moved from Miami. You know: the irony is not lost on you, but Cartagena costs roughly a third of what Miami does.

Saturday morning: walk through the city gate into the Walled City at 6:30am before the heat arrives. The plazas are quiet; the light on the yellow and ochre facades is extraordinary. Coffee from a cart at Plaza Trinidad. $1. A man is setting up a cumbia sound system for the afternoon. You make a note to come back.

Afternoon: a boat to the Rosario Islands with two friends — a Canadian couple who arrived a year before you. The coral reef snorkeling is excellent; the water is warm and clear. A seafood lunch on a floating restaurant. $35 for two including drinks.

Evening: back to Getsemaní. The neighborhood is alive at 8pm — cumbia from a bar, children playing in the plaza, the smell of arepas from a street cart. Dinner at a spot the locals use: bandeja paisa, a cold Club Colombia. $18 for two. This is Saturday in Cartagena. There are worse Saturdays.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Colombia's immigration and healthcare infrastructure has improved significantly. Here's what to know before moving.

🛂
Colombia Pensionado visa
Colombia's Pensionado visa (migrant category M) requires proof of a pension, Social Security, or retirement income of approximately $700/month — specifically, three times Colombia's minimum monthly wage (Salario Mínimo), which adjusts annually. The visa is granted initially for 3 years, is renewable, and after 5 continuous years of residence, qualifies for permanent residency (Residente R visa). Applications are filed online through Colombia's Cancillería, with processing times typically 4–6 weeks. An immigration attorney in Cartagena or Bogotá charges $400–700 and is highly recommended for navigating the documentation requirements.
🏥
Healthcare in Cartagena
Cartagena's private healthcare — Clínica Universitaria San Juan de Dios and Clínica El Bosque — handles most general, emergency, and specialist care well. For complex or unusual cases, Bogotá (1 hour by plane, or reachable by fast boat + highway) has significantly more depth: Fundación Santa Fe de Bogotá is internationally accredited and approaches US quality standards. Private health insurance in Colombia runs $1,500–4,000/year for comprehensive coverage. Many expats also enroll in Colombia's EPS (public health) system after obtaining residency — premiums are low but wait times apply for non-urgent care. US Medicare does not extend to Colombia.
🌡️
The heat — it is real
Cartagena sits at sea level on the Caribbean coast and is hot and humid year-round — average highs of 88–92°F, rarely below 78°F at night. This is not a "mild climate with warm days" situation. It's a tropical climate that requires genuine adaptation. Air conditioning is not optional in most months — it's a utility cost you must budget for. People who thrive here tend to be those who embrace the rhythm: outdoor activity in the early morning (before 9am), retreat to cool interiors during midday heat, re-emergence in the late afternoon and evening when the city comes back to life. This is the Caribbean way and it works, but it requires adjustment from temperate-climate habits.
