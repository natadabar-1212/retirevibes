Why Valencia might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

Spain's third-largest city gets less attention than Madrid and Barcelona — which is exactly why it's still affordable, still livable, and still feels like a real city rather than a tourist attraction.

🌊

Beach and city, genuinely combined

Valencia is the rare big city where you can cycle from the medieval old town to a real Mediterranean beach in 20 minutes. The Malvarrosa beachfront stretches for miles, there's a dedicated cycling lane the entire way, and the water is warm enough to swim in from May through October. Most beach cities are resort towns in disguise; Valencia is a real city first.

🥘

The food city in the food country

Paella was invented here. Not a tourist version of it — the original, cooked over orange wood in a flat pan, made with local rice from the Albufera lagoon just south of the city. The Mercado Central is one of the most beautiful covered markets in Europe, open every morning. Eating well in Valencia isn't expensive; it's just what happens when you live here.

🚲

A city built for bikes and pedestrians

The old riverbed running through the center of Valencia was converted into a 9km park — the Turia Garden — connecting the old city to the futuristic City of Arts and Sciences. Valencia has one of the best cycling infrastructures in Europe. The climate (300+ sunny days, almost no rain June–September) means you actually use it year-round.

💶

Europe without the European price tag

Valencia costs roughly 30% less than Madrid and 40% less than Barcelona — for the same EU lifestyle, the same healthcare system, the same visa path, the same high-speed rail connections. Groceries run 30% less than the Spanish capital. A nice dinner for two with wine is €35–50. The question isn't why Valencia — it's why anyone chooses the more expensive Spanish cities first.

What a Saturday in Valencia feels like.

The Mercado Central opens at 7am and you're there by 8 — buying a bag of clementines, a fresh bream, and a slice of turrón from a stall that's been in the same family for four generations. The light inside the market is amber and cathedral-like. Everyone is loud and cheerful in the way that Spanish markets always are.

Late morning: a coffee at the bar on the corner of Ruzafa, standing at the counter the way everyone does, paying 1.20€. Then a walk through the Barrio del Carmen — the medieval neighborhood inside the old walls, where the narrow lanes are covered in street art and the buildings lean so close together they nearly touch.

Afternoon: cycle out to the beach, swim, read, eat a late lunch of mussels and fried anchovies at a chiringuito with your feet in the sand. Back home through the Turia park as the light goes golden. You cook at home tonight. The clementines were excellent. Tomorrow you might do exactly the same thing.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Visa

Non-Lucrative Visa

Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV) is the standard path for American retirees — designed for people with passive income who won't be working in Spain. The 2026 income threshold is approximately €2,400/month (~$2,600) for a single applicant, plus 25% per dependent. It's worth knowing this is a higher bar than Portugal's D7. Apply through your nearest Spanish consulate 3–4 months before your target start date.

Healthcare

Private first, public after 5 years

The Non-Lucrative Visa requires private health insurance with no co-payments and no waiting periods — this is strictly checked at the consulate. Major providers for expats include Sanitas, Adeslas, and Asisa at €60–120/month per person. After gaining permanent residency (generally 5 years), you can access the Spanish public SNS healthcare system, which is among the best in Europe.

Taxes

US-Spain treaty — still get a CPA

The US and Spain have a tax treaty, but it doesn't eliminate all complexity. Spain taxes residents on worldwide income after 183 days in-country. Spain's Beckham Law tax regime (flat 24% for first 6 years) applies to certain new residents — worth understanding before you file. A cross-border CPA who knows both systems is essential, not optional, for Americans retiring here.
