Why Puerto Vallarta might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

Puerto Vallarta has been the definitive Mexican beach retirement town for Americans for 50 years. The infrastructure, the expat community, the direct flights, the beach — it all works. The question is which part of PV fits your pace.

🌅

Banderas Bay — one of the great bays in the world

Banderas Bay is 26 miles across, ringed by the Sierra Madre, and faces due west — which means the sunsets are genuinely spectacular every night. The bay is warm enough to swim year-round, calm enough for kayaking in the morning, and dramatic enough to never get old. The old town malecón runs along the water for a mile and a half. Whale watching season (December through March) brings humpbacks right into the bay. It's one of those places that actually looks like the photographs.

🏘️

A real city, not just a resort

Zona Romántica — the South Side — is the heart of the local PV that most expats end up in: cobblestone streets, independent restaurants, fruit markets, neighborhood taquerias, and an arts scene built around the Cuale River island. El Centro has the cathedral and the older, more Mexican-feeling part of town. Marina Vallarta and Nuevo Vallarta to the north offer modern condos and resort infrastructure. You can live here at whatever level of immersion you actually want.

✈️

Direct flights to half of the US

PVR (Licenciado Gustavo Díaz Ordaz International) has direct flights to LAX, SFO, SEA, ORD, DFW, PHX, ATL, IAH, JFK, and a dozen more US cities. Most West Coast flights are 3–4 hours. East Coast is 5–6. It's the most connected beach retirement town in Mexico — which matters when you're thinking about how often you'll visit family, or how often family might actually come to you.

🦞

The food, the culture, the community

PV has been building a restaurant scene for decades — it goes well beyond tourist tacos. There's a James Beard–recognized chef culture here, a serious art gallery scene, and a theater (IMAC Cultural Center). The American expat community is one of the oldest and most established in Mexico — tens of thousands of Americans have lived here full-time. There are English-language newspapers, social organizations, volunteer programs, English-speaking doctors and lawyers. The soft landing infrastructure is genuinely robust.

What a Thursday in Zona Romántica feels like.

You walk to the malecón before 8am, when it's quiet and the fishermen are still out. The light on the bay is the kind you don't get in most places — Banderas Bay catches it at a specific angle in the morning that you've never quite seen anywhere else. You stop at the corner coffee place you've been going to for two years. The woman who runs it knows your order.

Lunch is at a taquería three blocks from the condo — the one that's been there since the 1980s, cash only, $4 for three pastor tacos. The afternoon you work on a watercolor you've been painting, which is something you'd said for 25 years you'd do when you retired. You're doing it. Around 4pm you walk to the beach. December, 78 degrees, water warm.

Dinner is with six other couples you've met through the expat meetup — two from Colorado, one from Ohio, one originally from Mexico City. The restaurant is on a rooftop terrace over the old town. The cathedral is lit up across the square. The bay is dark and wide behind it all, and someone just spotted a whale spout offshore. This is the life you were trying to get to.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Visa

Residente Temporal — income bar has risen

Mexico's Residente Temporal visa is the standard expat residency path, but income requirements have increased significantly. As of 2026, many Mexican consulates are benchmarking around $4,400/month for temporary residency — up from previous years. Some consulates accept a savings-based alternative (~$140K+ USD). Requirements vary by consulate, so verify at the specific one you'll apply through. After four years as Temporal you can apply for Permanente, which has a higher income threshold at current consulate rates.

Healthcare

Strong private care, good English coverage

PV has a well-developed private medical infrastructure given its long history as an expat destination. CMQ Hospital, Hospital Amerimed, and several independent clinics offer English-speaking care. Specialist visits run $40–$60 without insurance. Private health insurance for two adults runs approximately $200–$340/month depending on age and coverage level. Legal residents can apply for IMSS (public coverage) but most expats rely on private plans for faster access and English service.

Taxes

US-Mexico treaty — but get a CPA anyway

Mexico taxes residents on Mexican-sourced income only — your US Social Security, pension, and US investment income is generally not taxed by Mexico. There is a US-Mexico tax treaty, which helps prevent double taxation. However, US citizens abroad still file US returns and report foreign accounts. The interaction of Mexican residency with US FBAR, FATCA, and potential state tax obligations requires a cross-border CPA who knows both systems. Don't try to navigate this one yourself.
