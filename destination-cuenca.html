Why People Choose Cuenca

The vibe in four cards

Cuenca has been on the expat radar for 20+ years — and the community that's built up here is part of the appeal. It's not a secret. It's a proven, well-trodden path with a lot of people who've already figured it out.

🏛️
UNESCO colonial beauty
Cuenca's historic center is a UNESCO World Heritage Site — Spanish colonial architecture, four rivers running through the city, the famous blue domes of the New Cathedral visible from almost everywhere. The streets are cobblestone, the plazas are lined with flowers, and the whole city has the feeling of a place that takes its own beauty seriously.
💵
USD with the lowest visa bar anywhere
Ecuador uses the US dollar — no exchange rate math, no currency risk. The Jubilado (retiree) visa requires just $800/month in pension or Social Security income. That's the lowest bar on our entire list. Approved in weeks, not months. Permanent residency path available after 3 years.
🌡️
Eternal spring — without the heat
At 8,400 feet, Cuenca is cooler than Costa Rica or Oaxaca — temperatures sit at 62–68°F year-round, with warm sunny afternoons and cool evenings. No AC needed. No heat pump needed. The climate is what most people describe as "perfect sweater weather" — spring, every day, forever.
👥
A built-in expat community
Cuenca has one of the most established North American expat communities in South America — there are English-language newspapers, expat social clubs, volunteering organizations, and entire neighborhoods (El Vergel area, nicknamed "Gringolandia") with infrastructure built around the community. If you want to arrive and immediately find your people, Cuenca makes that easy.

Life on the Ground

A Tuesday in the Athens of Ecuador

You live in a 2BR apartment near Parque Calderón — the central plaza in the historic district. Rent is $650/mo, utilities included. From your window you can see the blue domes of the New Cathedral against the Andes sky. The altitude took two weeks to adjust to. Now you don't notice it.

Tuesday morning is market day at Mercado 10 de Agosto. You buy a week of vegetables, fruit, fresh bread, and a bag of local coffee — $18. On the way home you stop at a small café run by a Cuencana who's been serving the same breakfast for twenty years: eggs, fresh juice, toast, café con leche — $3.50 for two.

Afternoons, you've been taking Spanish classes three times a week. Your level is intermediate now — enough to chat with neighbors, enough to negotiate your rent, enough to feel like you actually live here rather than touring. The class has eight people: three Americans, two Canadians, a couple from the UK. You've started meeting for drinks on Fridays.

This evening: a concert at the Teatro Sucre. Classical music, Ecuadorian composer. Tickets $8 each. You're home by 10pm and it's already 60°F and you sleep with the window open. You do this every night.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Ecuador is one of the most straightforward countries to retire to — but altitude, healthcare access, and a few other realities are worth knowing upfront.

📋
The Jubilado visa — the simplest path
Ecuador's Jubilado (retiree) visa requires $800/month in guaranteed income from a pension or Social Security. Applied for in Ecuador (not at a consulate) — you enter on a tourist visa and apply in-country. The process typically takes 2–6 weeks with an immigration attorney's help. After 3 years on the Jubilado visa, you can apply for permanent residency. Attorney fees: $1,000–2,000 all-in. It's genuinely one of the easiest retirement visa paths in the world.
🏥
Healthcare: good quality, lower cost
Cuenca has solid private hospitals — Hospital Monte Sinaí and Clínica Santa Ana are the top options, with English-speaking staff increasingly common. Specialist visits: $40–80. US Medicare does not cover you in Ecuador. International health insurance runs $100–250/mo depending on age. One important note: at 8,400 feet, altitude can worsen certain cardiovascular or respiratory conditions — consult your doctor before committing if this is relevant to you.
💰
Altitude and taxes
Altitude adjustment: most people adapt within 2–3 weeks; symptoms include fatigue, shortness of breath, and headaches initially. If you have heart or lung conditions, get a medical opinion first. On taxes: Ecuador does not tax foreign-sourced income, so US SS, pension, and investment income are not taxed by Ecuador. There is no US-Ecuador tax treaty, so you still file US taxes normally. An expat CPA helps ensure FBAR and other filings are handled; budget $400–800/yr.
