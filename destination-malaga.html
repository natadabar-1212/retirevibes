Why People Choose Málaga

The vibe in four cards

Málaga used to be a transit point on the way to beach resorts. It isn't anymore. The city has undergone a genuine cultural awakening — and retirees who got here early are still congratulating themselves.

☀️
The most sunshine in Europe
Málaga logs 320+ sunny days per year — more than any other major city in continental Europe. Winters are genuinely mild (55–65°F), summers are hot (85–95°F) but bearable with the sea breeze. If weather drove you to Arizona, this is its European equivalent — with ocean.
🎨
Picasso, the Pompidou, and a reinvented city
Málaga is Picasso's birthplace — the Picasso Museum sits in his family's neighborhood. The city now has a satellite of the Centre Pompidou, a branch of the Russian State Museum, and a thriving Soho arts district that's attracted galleries and studios from across Spain. This is not a sleepy beach town.
🏖️
Beach at the end of a short walk
Malagueta Beach is a 15-minute walk from the historic center. Benalmádena, Torremolinos, and dozens of Costa del Sol beach towns are 15–30 minutes away by commuter train. You can live in a walkable urban neighborhood and have the sand whenever you want it.
✈️
Direct flights from the US — and expanding
Málaga-Costa del Sol Airport (AGP) is growing its transatlantic routes. Direct flights from New York, Boston, and Philadelphia are now running seasonally, with more expected. Madrid is 2.5 hours by high-speed train — putting you one connection from anywhere in Europe.

Life on the Ground

A Wednesday evening in Málaga

You wake up in your apartment in Soho — 2BR, €950/mo, ten minutes' walk to the Picasso Museum. The mornings here are what people dream about: mild air, the sound of the city waking up, strong café solo at the bar downstairs for €1.20.

Wednesday afternoons you walk to Malagueta Beach. It's November and there are 30 people on the sand — mostly locals. You swim. A retiree from Seattle waves from a beach chair. You've met him three times now. He moved here two years ago and says the adjustment took about a month. Now he can't imagine leaving.

Dinner in the evening is tapas in El Palo, the fishing neighborhood east of the center. A plate of fried pescaíto, glass of Malaga wine, conversation in broken Spanish with the couple at the next table — €22 for everything. You walk home along the Paseo Marítimo with the lights of the city reflected in the water.

Tomorrow you have a Spanish class at 10am. You've been here six months and you're starting to feel like you actually live here — not like you're visiting indefinitely.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa is a genuinely good option for retirees — but it requires preparation. Here's what to know.

📋
Spain's Non-Lucrative Visa (NLV)
Spain's path for retirees who don't plan to work in Spain. You must demonstrate approximately €2,400/mo in passive income (pension, SS, investments) — currently around $2,600/mo at recent rates — plus mandatory private health insurance covering Spain. Applied for at a Spanish consulate in the US. Renewed annually; after 5 years you can apply for long-term residency. Note: you cannot work for Spanish entities on this visa.
🏥
Healthcare: good private system
Spain's public healthcare is excellent — but NLV holders must use private insurance (required for the visa). Sanitas, Adeslas, and Asisa are the main private providers at €100–250/mo depending on age and coverage level. Hospital Quirónsalud Málaga and Hospital HM Málaga are English-friendly private facilities. US Medicare does not cover you in Spain.
💰
Taxes: Beckham Law and the US treaty
The US-Spain tax treaty exists, which helps. Most retirees pay Spanish income tax on their worldwide income once they become tax residents (183+ days/yr). Spain's Beckham Law (favorable flat rate for new residents) may apply in some situations — consult a Spanish tax advisor (gestor). Spain taxes capital gains and dividends. A cross-border CPA is worth the investment here; budget €800–1,500/yr.
