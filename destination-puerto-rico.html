Why Puerto Rico might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

For Americans who want the feel of going somewhere genuinely different — tropical, Latin, Caribbean — without the visa process, the Medicare gap, or the flight that takes 18 hours, Puerto Rico is the answer.

🛂

No visa. No immigration. Your Medicare works.

Puerto Rico is a US territory — US citizens move there the same way they move to Florida. No immigration attorney, no income documentation for a consulate, no renewal every two years. Your Medicare Parts A and B work there exactly as they do on the mainland. Social Security is paid the same way. If the visa and insurance bureaucracy of an international move feels like a dealbreaker, Puerto Rico removes it entirely. You're choosing a lifestyle, not navigating a foreign government.

✈️

Three hours to the mainland — real proximity

Luis Muñoz Marín International Airport (SJU) in San Juan has direct flights to New York (3 hrs), Miami (2.5 hrs), Chicago, Boston, Philadelphia, Atlanta, and more. There are also regional airports on the west coast (BQN near Aguadilla/Rincón) with direct service to major East Coast cities. That proximity is the thing that makes Puerto Rico feel different from every other tropical retirement option: you can be with your grandchildren for a weekend with one flight that doesn't require a full day of travel.

🌴

Genuinely tropical — and genuinely different

Puerto Rico averages 80–85°F year-round with trade winds that keep it livable. Old San Juan is a UNESCO-recognized historic district — 500-year-old Spanish colonial architecture in cobblestone blue — and one of the most beautiful small cities in the Americas. The west coast (Rincón, Aguadilla, Isabela) is a surf town with a strong American expat community. El Yunque is the only tropical rainforest in the US National Forest system. This is not just another warm beach — it's a place with real character.

💰

Meaningfully cheaper than the US mainland

Puerto Rico's cost of living sits roughly 10–20% below the US national average, and 30–50% below coastal metros like New York, Boston, or San Francisco. Housing in particular runs significantly lower: a nice 2BR apartment in a good San Juan neighborhood costs what a 1BR in a mid-tier US city would. The Act 60 tax incentives (formerly Act 20/22) can reduce Puerto Rico income tax significantly for eligible newcomers — worth exploring with a CPA if you have investment or business income. The island is not free, but it's meaningfully less expensive than the place most people are moving from.

What a Friday in Old San Juan feels like.

The morning starts early because the light comes through the window differently here — the Caribbean has a particular quality to it, brighter and more horizontal than anywhere on the East Coast. You walk out through the blue cobblestones to the Paseo de la Princesa, past the old city walls. The fortifications are 500 years old and still standing. There are joggers. There is a coffee cart that you've been going to every morning for three months.

You video call your daughter in Boston before lunch. She's coming in six weeks. You've already planned the itinerary: Castillo San Felipe del Morro, the beach at Condado, a day trip to the bioluminescent bay in Vieques. You're the one who knows the island now. That's a new feeling. Lunch is mofongo at the place two blocks from your apartment — $14, better than anything you'd find in the States.

Afternoon you sit at the café on Calle del Cristo with the wifi and your book. At 5pm the light goes gold on the buildings and everyone who lives here comes outside. This is the hour the city is made for. Dinner later with the couple you met at salsa class — a retired teacher from Connecticut and her husband, who didn't know anyone when they moved six months ago and now seem to know everyone.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Residency & taxes

Act 60 — worth knowing about

Moving to Puerto Rico as a US citizen requires no visa or immigration process — you're moving between US territories. For taxes: Puerto Rico has its own tax system, and bona fide Puerto Rico residents pay Puerto Rico income tax (not federal income tax on Puerto Rico-sourced income). Act 60 (formerly Acts 20 and 22) offers a flat 4% corporate rate and 0% capital gains tax on qualifying income for new residents who meet annual charity contribution and presence requirements. The rules are specific and change — get a Puerto Rico CPA before assuming you qualify.

Healthcare

Medicare works — plan your coverage

Medicare Parts A and B are accepted by most hospitals and providers in Puerto Rico. Medicare Advantage plans are available and widely used — several major insurers offer island-specific plans. Presbyterian Medical Center in San Juan is the flagship hospital and accepts Medicare; specialist availability is concentrated in the San Juan metro area. If you're planning to live on the west coast or in a rural area, confirm your specialist needs before committing — primary care is widely available but some specialties are San Juan–centric.

Infrastructure

The grid — better, but worth knowing

Puerto Rico's power grid has improved significantly since Hurricane Maria (2017) and a series of subsequent earthquakes and storms, but it remains less reliable than the mainland US. Urban areas rarely experience extended outages; rural areas occasionally do. Many residents have solar + battery systems (LUMA Energy and the territory have expanded solar infrastructure). Internet is generally reliable in populated areas with fiber from Liberty and other providers. This is real information, not a dealbreaker — just worth factoring in for any remote work or medical equipment needs.
