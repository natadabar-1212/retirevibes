Abruzzo, Italy

Italy at half the price of Tuscany — medieval hill villages, Apennine ski slopes, Adriatic beaches, and some of Italy's least-discovered wine country.

The Vibe

The Italy that Italians kept to themselves.

Abruzzo is what Italy looked like before the tour buses arrived. Medieval villages clinging to hillsides above valleys that still look exactly as they did in the 15th century. Wolves and bears in the national park — the only place in Western Europe where both coexist in the wild. Saffron fields outside the village of Navelli, where the world's most expensive spice has been harvested since the 13th century. And an Adriatic coastline 45 minutes from ski slopes in the Gran Sasso, which makes Abruzzo among the only places in the world where you can ski in the morning and swim in the afternoon on the same day.

The cost story is straightforward: Abruzzo costs roughly half of Tuscany or Rome. Property prices in hill villages are extraordinary — a fully restored 3-bedroom stone house can be found for $150,000–$200,000 in many towns. The Italian government has also been running a €1 home program in depopulating villages across the region, where homes are essentially given away to buyers who commit to renovation.

Italy is not the easiest country to navigate bureaucratically, and Abruzzo is less internationally connected than Rome or Milan. But for people who want the real Italy — the version where the neighbors know your name and lunch takes two hours — Abruzzo delivers an experience that money genuinely cannot buy in more famous regions.

Housing

What you'll pay to live here

Abruzzo has some of the most affordable property prices in all of Italy. The hill village market has been attracting foreign buyers — particularly from the UK, US, and Germany — for the past decade as word has gradually spread.

Expat note: EU citizens can buy property in Italy freely. Americans purchase directly but must navigate Italian bureaucracy — an Italian notaio (notary) handles all property transfers, and the process typically takes 3–6 months. Italy's Elective Residency Visa is a popular path for retirees who can show passive income of roughly €2,000/month (individual) or €3,000/month (couple). Consult an Italian immigration attorney and a US international tax advisor before purchasing — Italy has complex double-taxation considerations.
Where to Spend Time

Three zones worth exploring

Abruzzo is a full region, not a single city — understanding its distinct zones tells you which kind of Abruzzo life fits you best.

The Adriatic Coast — Pescara, Lanciano, Vasto

The coastal strip gives you beaches, the regional capital's infrastructure, and the most practical baseline for everyday life. Pescara is Abruzzo's largest city — where the hospitals are, where the airport is, where you'd go for anything you can't find in a village. Not charming in the postcard sense, but important to understand. Lanciano and Vasto are more attractive coastal alternatives, with historic centers, good markets, and enough expat presence to find English speakers. Drive the coast road on a spring afternoon to understand why people choose this over the hill villages.

The Hill Villages — Civitella del Tronto, Guardiagrele, Lanciano

This is the Abruzzo that draws international buyers. Medieval fortified towns with views over valleys that seem unchanged for centuries. Civitella del Tronto holds one of Italy's best-preserved fortresses and a small but active international community. Guardiagrele is known for its goldsmiths and artisan culture. Spend several days driving between these villages — the pace is entirely different from anything coastal, and it tells you immediately whether this is your Italy or not.

The Mountains — L'Aquila, Gran Sasso, Majella

L'Aquila is Abruzzo's historic mountain capital, still recovering from the 2009 earthquake but with a remarkable rebuilt historic center and a university that keeps it young. The Gran Sasso massif — the highest point in the Apennines at 2,912 meters — provides skiing in winter and hiking in summer from a base that's still only 90 minutes from Rome. For retirees drawn to mountain living, serious outdoor access, and cooler summers, the inland highlands are worth at least several days of a scouting trip.
