🇨🇦 Vancouver, BC, Canada

Mountains, ocean, Stanley Park, and one of the world's great food cities

Vancouver regularly tops global livability rankings — and having been there, you understand why. The North Shore mountains rise directly behind the city. The Pacific is everywhere. Stanley Park, a 400-hectare old-growth forest with a 22km seawall, begins at the edge of downtown. The food scene — shaped by the largest Chinese-Canadian community outside Asia, Japanese, Korean, South Asian, and Pacific Northwest culinary traditions — is one of the finest on the continent. The winters are mild by Canadian standards. The housing costs are not.

Why People Choose Vancouver

The vibe in four cards

Vancouver is for people who want genuine world-class urban infrastructure — food, culture, healthcare, transit — combined with immediate wilderness access that no comparable city on earth can match.

🌲
Stanley Park and urban wilderness
Stanley Park is 405 hectares of old-growth temperate rainforest on a peninsula at the edge of downtown Vancouver — one of the largest urban parks in North America. The 22km seawall circumnavigating the park is a world-class walking, cycling, and running route with views of English Bay, the North Shore mountains, and Lions Gate Bridge. Inside the park: Beaver Lake, ancient western red cedar and Douglas fir trees, Great Blue Heron colony, and forest trails that feel genuinely wild within 10 minutes of downtown. The North Shore mountains (Grouse Mountain, Cypress Mountain, Mount Seymour) are visible from virtually everywhere in the city, accessible by car in 30–45 minutes, and offer skiing in winter and hiking in summer. Garibaldi Provincial Park — 2 hours north — is one of BC's finest wilderness areas, with alpine lakes and glacier access. Vancouver's geography provides a constant visual and physical relationship with wilderness that is unique among major world cities.
🍜
The food scene — one of North America's finest
Vancouver's food scene is shaped by geography and demographics: the Pacific Ocean (Dungeness crab, wild salmon, halibut, spot prawns), the Fraser Valley (exceptional produce, dairy, poultry), and one of the most diverse populations in Canada. Richmond — the suburb directly south of Vancouver — has the highest concentration of authentic Chinese restaurants outside mainland China, with Hong Kong–style dim sum, Cantonese seafood, Sichuan hot pot, and Taiwanese beef noodle soup at a quality and price that is extraordinary. Japanese food: Miku's aburi (flame-seared) sushi, Tojo's kaiseki, Ramen Danbo, and Japadog hot dogs (a Vancouver original, now globally known). Pacific Northwest cuisine: Bishop's, Hawksworth, and the farm-to-table movement running on Okanagan produce and Pacific seafood. The Granville Island Public Market is the city's kitchen — cheese, charcuterie, fresh pasta, Pacific salmon, baked goods — in a converted industrial space under the Granville Bridge. For people who organize their lives around food, Vancouver delivers on every front simultaneously.
🎭
Arts, culture, and the Vancouver character
Vancouver has a strong arts and cultural infrastructure for a city of its size — the Vancouver Art Gallery (strong collection of Emily Carr and Group of Seven Pacific Northwest painters), Vancouver Opera, Vancouver Symphony Orchestra, Arts Club Theatre Company, and a film industry that has made it one of the most filmed cities in North America (the "Hollywood North" sobriquet is apt). The Bard on the Beach Shakespeare Festival runs in tents on the beach in Vanier Park from June through September — one of the finest outdoor Shakespeare programs in North America. The Museum of Anthropology at UBC has one of the world's greatest collections of Northwest Coast Indigenous art. The city's cultural character is West Coast — relaxed, outdoors-oriented, multicultural, and somewhat informal compared to Toronto or Montreal. The result is a place with genuine urban cultural depth that doesn't take itself too seriously.
🚡
World-class transit and walkable neighbourhoods
Vancouver's SkyTrain rapid transit network — driverless, elevated and underground, covering the city and reaching the airport — is one of North America's best urban transit systems. Combined with the extensive bus network, the Aquabus and False Creek Ferries (small commuter water taxis crossing False Creek), and the West Coast Express commuter rail to the Fraser Valley, Vancouver's transit serves a large portion of daily needs for residents who choose to live near a station. The walkability of individual neighbourhoods — Kitsilano, Main Street, Commercial Drive ("The Drive"), West End, Mount Pleasant — is exceptional: each has its own character, local restaurants, independent coffee shops, weekend markets, and park spaces. The West End, the residential neighbourhood immediately adjacent to Stanley Park, is one of the most walkable and livable urban neighbourhoods in Canada: dense, park-adjacent, transit-served, diverse, and surprisingly affordable relative to the surrounding city.

Life on the Ground

A Saturday between the mountains and the market

You rent a 1BR apartment in Kitsilano — CAD $2,800/month, third floor, view of English Bay and the North Shore mountains from the kitchen window. You moved from San Francisco. The housing is roughly the same price. Everything else is better.

8am: the Kits Beach seawall south toward the Burrard Bridge and back. The mountains are out — clear after yesterday's rain, the snow line just above the mid-slopes of Cypress. You have run this route 200 times. You still stop at the same bench to look at the mountains. You can't help it.

Midmorning: Granville Island Market. Fresh spot prawns from the fishmonger — they came in this morning from the Strait of Georgia, still alive in the tank. You buy a pound. Cowichan Valley cheese, sourdough, and a container of fresh pasta from the Italian vendor. CAD $65 for the whole shop.

Afternoon: Grouse Mountain. The gondola up is CAD $65; the trail (the Grouse Grind — 2.9km, 853m elevation gain, a genuinely hard hike) is free. You take the trail. You're up in 75 minutes. Beer at the summit brewpub. The city is below you and the mountains go north forever and you think: yes, this is why.

The Numbers

What it actually costs

Vancouver is one of the most expensive cities in North America for housing — routinely ranking alongside San Francisco, New York, and Hong Kong. The daily cost of living outside housing is more moderate, and the CAD/USD exchange provides a meaningful offset for Americans.

Vancouver's housing market is one of the world's most expensive — median detached home price exceeds CAD $2M in the city proper; condos are the practical option for most retirees. BC levies a foreign buyer property transfer tax (20%) on residential purchases in Metro Vancouver by non-Canadian buyers — this applies to Americans purchasing as non-residents and significantly affects the purchase economics. Renting is often the more practical choice for Americans without permanent residence status. Whether this fits your plan is your call — these are facts on the ground, not a recommendation.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Same visa reality as all Canadian cities — no retirement visa, 6 months per year as a visitor. Vancouver adds a significant foreign buyer tax consideration for property purchases.

🛂
Immigration and the 6-month reality
Americans can visit Canada for up to 6 months (183 days) per year without a visa — many American retirees use this to spend Canadian summers in Vancouver (June–September is spectacular) and return to the US for the winter. Full-time residence requires immigration through Canada's Express Entry or Provincial Nominee Program — both designed primarily for working-age people. BC's Provincial Nominee Program has streams for investors and entrepreneurs, but a passive-income retirement pathway does not exist in the Canadian system. For Americans with Canadian spouses or family connections, sponsorship pathways exist. For others without these connections, the 6-months-per-year pattern is the practical reality. The positive view: Vancouver summers (June–September) are arguably the finest urban outdoor experience in North America — consistently mild at 20–25°C, no humidity, mountains clear, days long. Many Americans find a seasonal Vancouver presence deeply satisfying without pursuing full immigration.
🏠
Foreign buyer tax — a critical consideration
BC's Additional Property Transfer Tax applies a 20% tax on the purchase price of residential property by foreign nationals (non-Canadian citizens and non-permanent residents) in Metro Vancouver, the Fraser Valley, the Capital Regional District (Victoria), and several other BC regions. For a CAD $900,000 condo, this adds CAD $180,000 in tax on top of the purchase price — a significant cost. Americans who purchase as visitors (not permanent residents) are subject to this tax. Americans who achieve BC permanent residence through Canadian immigration are not. This tax fundamentally changes the rent-vs-buy calculation for American visitors in Vancouver; most financial advisors recommend that non-resident Americans in Vancouver rent rather than buy, at least until they achieve permanent residence status. The rental market is strong and well-supplied with professionally managed inventory.
🌧️
The rain — honest accounting
Vancouver has approximately 165 days of precipitation per year — concentrated in November through March, when weeks of grey, drizzly Pacific weather are the norm. The winters are mild (rarely below 0°C in the city, though the mountains get heavy snow) but persistently grey and damp. The people who love Vancouver's winter are those who embrace indoor culture (the restaurant and coffee scene thrives in the rain), outdoor activities that don't require sun (hiking in the rain, running in the rain, skiing in the mountains when it's clear up top), and the fact that January in Vancouver at 7°C is not January in Calgary at -15°C. The people who struggle are those who need regular sunshine for mood and energy. This is a genuine and common experience — Seasonal Affective Disorder is not uncommon among Vancouver residents. The solution many long-term Vancouverites use: travel south (Palm Springs, Mexico) for 3–4 weeks in February, the darkest point of the year. The summers, which are reliably sunny and warm from June through September, are among the finest in Canada and fully compensate for the winter in most people's accounting.

Places with a similar vibe

Pacific coast, mountains and ocean together, world-class food, walkable urban life. These share Vancouver's spirit.

🇨🇦
Victoria, BC
Vancouver Island
Same Pacific climate and BC character, 90 minutes by ferry, significantly less expensive for housing. Less urban intensity — smaller city with a more relaxed pace. Often described as Vancouver for people who want less of it. Same immigration constraints apply.
🇨🇦
Kelowna, BC
Okanagan Valley
BC's wine country interior — 4 hours east of Vancouver, hot dry summers and mild winters, Okanagan Lake, and housing costs roughly half of Vancouver's. Less urban; more outdoor and wine-focused lifestyle. The foreign buyer tax does not apply in Kelowna as it does in Metro Vancouver.
🇨🇦
Nanaimo, BC
Vancouver Island
Vancouver Island's second city — affordable, ferry-connected to the mainland, outdoors-focused, and growing in amenities. No foreign buyer tax. Housing costs well below both Vancouver and Victoria. Less cultural density but immediate access to hiking, cycling, and ocean.
