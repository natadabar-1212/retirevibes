Why People Choose Boise

The vibe in four cards

Boise has quietly become one of the most livable cities in the West — not because it's been discovered, but because people who move here keep not leaving.

🚴
The Greenbelt — 25 miles of river path
The Boise River Greenbelt is one of the great urban trails in America — 25 continuous miles of paved path along the river, through parks, past cottonwood groves, connecting neighborhoods without a traffic light. Cyclists, walkers, runners, and fishers use it year-round. For retirees who want daily movement built into the city itself, the Greenbelt delivers.
⛷️
Skiing 16 miles from downtown
Bogus Basin ski resort is 16 miles from downtown Boise — a 30-minute drive to 2,600 acres of skiing and snowboarding. For a city of 240,000 people, that access is extraordinary. Sun Valley (world-class resort) is 2.5 hours east. The Sawtooth Wilderness — one of the most spectacular landscapes in the US — is 2 hours away. Boise is a trailhead, not just a city.
🍷
A surprising food and arts scene
The Basque Block — a historic district that reflects Boise's unique Basque heritage — has restaurants, a cultural center, and a fronton pelota court. The Boise Contemporary Theater, Treefort Music Festival, and a genuine farm-to-table restaurant scene have made the city a cultural destination that keeps surprising people who write it off as flyover country.
🌡️
Manageable four seasons
Boise gets 206 sunny days per year — more than Seattle and Portland combined. Summers are warm (85–95°F) but dry and not humid. Winters are real but not extreme — snow comes, stays a few days, and melts. It doesn't have the brutal cold of the Midwest or the oppressive heat of Arizona. Four seasons, comfortably calibrated.

Life on the Ground

An October morning in Boise

You live in the North End — a historic neighborhood of craftsman bungalows, tree-lined streets, and a walkable stretch of restaurants and independent shops on 13th Street. Your 2BR home cost $560K. The cottonwoods along the Greenbelt behind the neighborhood are turning gold.

Morning: you cycle the Greenbelt west toward Ann Morrison Park, then back. 12 miles, 50 minutes. The river is low and clear this time of year, and you see a great blue heron at the same spot you always do. You've stopped photographing it. You're back at the house before 9am.

Afternoon: lunch at a Basque restaurant on the Basque Block — lamb stew, crusty bread, a glass of txakoli. $18. In the evening, there's a string quartet at the Treefort venue downtown. $15 tickets. You run into the neighbors you met at the farmer's market in August. They moved here from Portland. They say the same thing everyone says: they can't believe they waited this long.

Bogus Basin opened last week. You're going Sunday. The winter is coming, and you're ready for it.

Before You Go

The practical stuff

Idaho is a relatively simple domestic retirement move. A few things worth knowing.

💰
Idaho's retirement tax picture
Idaho has a flat income tax rate of 5.8% — not the lowest, but paired with low property taxes and no estate tax. Social Security income is partially exempt: Idaho exempts SS benefits from state income tax for residents over 65 with income under certain thresholds. Pension income has a deduction. Property taxes are relatively low — Boise homeowners benefit from the homeowner's exemption. Overall a middle-tier tax state for retirees.
🏥
Healthcare: two strong regional systems
St. Luke's Health System is the largest employer in Idaho and operates strong hospitals throughout the Boise metro. Saint Alphonsus (part of Trinity Health) is the other major system. Both are well-regarded regional networks with broad specialty coverage. For complex cases, Seattle's Virginia Mason and Portland's OHSU are 6–8 hours away. Medicare works at all facilities. Boise's healthcare is better than its size would suggest.
📈
A city that's been growing fast
Boise has been one of the fastest-growing metros in the US — home prices have roughly doubled since 2019 before moderating. That growth has brought more amenities, more restaurants, more cultural offerings — but also more traffic and higher costs than the Boise of ten years ago. The city has good bones and a strong quality of life, but go in understanding it's changed. The outdoor access hasn't — that's still extraordinary.
