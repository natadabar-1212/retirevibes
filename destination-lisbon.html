Why Lisbon might be your place

Four reasons this came up at the top.

Europe's most livable capital — 300 sunny days a year, Atlantic beaches within 30 minutes, and a city that rewards slow, wandering exploration over efficient tourism.

☀️

The sunniest capital in Europe

Lisbon averages 300 sunny days a year — more than anywhere else on the continent. Mild winters (rarely below 50°F), warm summers without the brutal heat of southern Spain, and an Atlantic breeze that softens everything. This is the climate people move to Portugal for.

✈️

Direct flights to the US

Humberto Delgado Airport has nonstop service to a dozen US cities — New York, Boston, Miami, Newark, Washington, Chicago. For most Americans, Lisbon is a 6–7 hour flight with no connections. That single fact changes the "can I really do this?" calculation entirely.

🚋

A city built for wandering

Seven hills, vintage trams, miradouros (viewpoints) that stop you mid-climb, and neighborhoods that each feel like their own village. Príncipe Real for antique shops and wine bars. Belém for riverside history. Campo de Ourique for the market and the slow Saturday morning. Cars are optional — the metro, bus, and tram network covers everything.

🌊

Beach access without beach prices

Cascais and Estoril are 40 minutes by train — some of the best beaches in Europe, with the drama of Atlantic surf rather than the crowded stillness of the Mediterranean. Sintra, a UNESCO world heritage site of fairy-tale palaces and misty forests, is 45 minutes the other direction. Both are on the same transit pass.

What a Wednesday in Lisbon feels like.

Morning light arrives sideways through the shutters and hits the azulejo tiles on the building across the street — blue and white, the way everything in Lisbon is eventually blue and white. You make coffee at home first. The café downstairs is for reading, not rushing.

Mid-morning walk through Príncipe Real to the organic market — blood oranges, queijo fresco, a jar of local honey. The woman at the honey stall doesn't speak English and you don't speak much Portuguese yet, and somehow everything gets sorted. This happens all the time here.

Lunch stretches until 3pm at a wine bar on a steep cobbled street. €22, including two glasses of Alentejo red and a dessert you didn't order but arrived anyway. The afternoon is yours — maybe the miradouro, maybe a tram ride to Belém to watch the river. You're home by sunset. The light is copper-gold on the tile rooftops. You put on music and open the windows and think: I could do this every day.

The practical stuff

Three things to know before you go further.

Visa

D7 retirement visa

Portugal's D7 visa is the standard path for American retirees — designed for people with stable passive income (pension, Social Security, dividends). The 2026 income threshold is €920/month for a single applicant, plus 50% per dependent. Initial residency for two years, renewable. Permanent residency at year 5. Budget 6–9 months for the full process.

Healthcare

SNS + private hybrid

As a resident, you get access to Portugal's public SNS system. Most American expats pair it with private insurance (€60–125/mo per person) for faster specialist appointments and guaranteed English-speaking doctors. Lisbon has excellent private hospitals including Hospital da Luz and CUF Descobertas — comparable to any major European city.

Compare insurance options →
Taxes

NHR is gone — talk to a CPA

Portugal's Non-Habitual Resident program — the famous 10% pension tax break — closed fully in early 2025. Its replacement, IFICI, targets researchers and tech professionals, not retirees. Standard Portuguese income tax applies at progressive rates. Portugal still has a US tax treaty, but the old NHR advantages are over. Get advice from a cross-border CPA before making any residency decisions.
