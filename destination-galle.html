Why Galle

The fort at the edge of the world

Galle Fort is one of the most extraordinary places in Asia. Built by the Portuguese in 1588 and expanded by the Dutch in the 17th century, it's a UNESCO World Heritage site that still functions as a living neighborhood — with boutique hotels in colonial-era townhouses, excellent restaurants, art galleries, yoga studios, and a local community of Sri Lankan families and a growing cohort of expats who've chosen it as their permanent home.

The fort sits on a headland where the Indian Ocean meets Sri Lanka's southern coast. You can walk the rampart walls at sunset and watch the sea in three directions. Jungle Beach — one of the island's hidden gems — is a ten-minute walk. Whales breach offshore from November to April. Sea turtles nest on the beaches. This is a place of profound natural beauty, and the cost of living is a fraction of anything comparable in Europe or North America.

Sri Lanka is still early on the expat curve. The infrastructure is improving, the hospitality culture is genuinely warm, and those who've arrived in the past decade have found a place that feels undiscovered in the best possible way.

The Vibe

What life actually feels like

🏯

Living Inside the Fort

The fort's narrow cobblestone streets are lined with colonial townhouses repainted in cream, white, and soft blue. Life is walkable, quiet, and deeply atmospheric. Church Street, Pedlar Street, and the waterfront promenade feel like a different century entirely. Expats who live here tend to stay — the fort gets into you.

🐋

Nature at Every Turn

Whale watching from November to April, sea turtle nesting on nearby Rekawa Beach, leopards in Yala National Park (2 hours east), and surfing at Hikkaduwa (18km). For retirees who want to feel connected to the natural world on a daily basis, the south coast of Sri Lanka is one of the most generously gifted places on earth.

🍛

Sri Lankan Food Is Underrated Everywhere

Hoppers (bowl-shaped fermented rice pancakes) with sambol and curry for $1.50 at a local joint. Rice and curry — a different sequence of a dozen dishes every day — for $2. Fresh seafood bought at the fishing harbor for pennies per kilo. Sri Lankan cuisine is complex, spiced, and deeply satisfying. The fort also has excellent cafés and internationally influenced restaurants from expat chefs.

🌅

Rampart Walks at Sunset

The defining daily ritual in Galle is walking the fort's ocean-facing ramparts as the sun goes down. The walls are wide enough to stroll in pairs, the Indian Ocean stretches in every direction, and the old lighthouse stands against a sky that turns coral and gold. It never gets old. Expats have been doing this walk for years and still stop to take photographs.

A Day in the Life

Your Friday in Galle

6:45 am

Hoppers & Fishing Harbor

Breakfast at a local hopper shop near the bus station — egg hopper, coconut sambol, dhal, and a pot of Ceylon tea. Then a walk to the fishing harbor to watch the multicolored oruwa outrigger boats come in with the morning catch. You're back home before the heat builds, with fresh kingfish for dinner wrapped in banana leaf.

10:00 am

Fort Life & Gallery Visits

A wander through the fort — the Dutch Reformed Church, the lighthouse, the rows of boutique galleries selling batik, gemstones, and contemporary Sri Lankan art. Coffee at a fort café, then an hour of reading in your courtyard. The sounds: a tuk-tuk, a muezzin, distant surf, a cuckoo in the frangipani tree.

5:00 pm

Rampart Walk & Seafood Dinner

The daily ritual: the rampart walk as the sun drops behind the lighthouse. You stop to chat with the expat couple who moved from Bath three years ago. Then dinner — the kingfish from this morning, grilled with turmeric and lime, with coconut roti. A glass of Sri Lankan arrack on the veranda. The ocean below, glittering.

Where to Live

Neighborhoods worth knowing

Most Iconic

Galle Fort

Inside the UNESCO-listed walls. Colonial townhouses converted to boutique rentals, cobblestone streets, the ocean on three sides. The most atmospheric address in Sri Lanka. Premium-priced by local standards, with waiting lists for the best properties.

Beach & Surf

Unawatuna

A 5-minute tuk-tuk from the fort, Unawatuna has a gentle bay beach, excellent dive sites, and a relaxed beach village atmosphere. Popular with younger expats and digital nomads, with great restaurants and a warm community.

Local & Spacious

Weligama / Mirissa

Further east (30–45 min), these surf towns offer more space, lower prices, and extraordinary whale-watching access. Weligama Bay is gentle enough for beginner surfers; Mirissa is where the blue whale boats depart. A quieter alternative for those who want more room.

Healthcare

Local Clinics & Colombo Specialists

Galle has the government Karapitiya Teaching Hospital, a large regional facility, plus several private clinics serving the expat community. For specialist care and surgery, Colombo (1.5 hours north) has Sri Lanka's best private hospitals — Lanka Hospitals, Nawaloka, and the Asiri group — with English-speaking specialists and modern facilities.

Most expats carry international health insurance. Medical costs in Sri Lanka are low — a private consultation runs $15–35 USD, and even major procedures are dramatically less expensive than in Western countries. Healthcare quality in Colombo's top hospitals is solid, though the range of specialist subspecialties is narrower than in Bangkok or KL.

Visas & Residency

Retirement Visa & Investor Path

Sri Lanka offers a Retirement Visa (Residence Visa for retirees) for those 55 and older. It requires evidence of monthly income of at least $1,500 USD (or a lump-sum bank deposit of ~$18,000 USD held in a Sri Lankan account). The visa is issued for 5 years and is renewable. It permits full-time residence but not employment.

Sri Lanka also offers a Resident Guest scheme and investor visa paths for those purchasing property or making a qualifying investment. The country is updating its long-stay visa framework — a local immigration attorney is the most reliable guide to current requirements, especially given Sri Lanka's recent economic recovery period.
